\title{A well-ranked score that certifies nothing:\\
selective risk control for document-level event-argument extraction}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Selective prediction is usually judged by how well a confidence score \emph{ranks} correct outputs
above incorrect ones --- AUROC, AURC, rank correlation. Distribution-free risk control asks a
different question: how much output can be accepted while provably bounding error on what is
accepted. We show these can diverge by an order of magnitude on the same system. We define a
frozen, sealed-split selective-certification protocol for document-level event-argument extraction
on RAMS, with the source document as the independent unit, and run it once on a QLoRA-adapted
Qwen3-4B and two prompted baselines. All three are infeasible at a 20\% risk target with 95\%
confidence and a 20\% acceptance floor, and the infeasibility is \textbf{overdetermined}: base
risk, ranking quality and the finite-sample penalty each independently forbid the target. The
adapted system's confidence score is \emph{not} weak by selective-prediction standards --- group
AUROC 0.796, Spearman $\rho = -0.403$, matching what log-probability achieves on structured
generation elsewhere --- and it certifies \textbf{nothing at all}. Nor would a perfect ranking:
ranked by true loss, the same population certifies 11.4\%, still short of the floor. Separating the
three mechanisms is what makes the null diagnostic rather than merely negative, and only one of
them is the score's fault.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Two literatures measure confidence and rarely meet. Selective prediction and hallucination
detection report \textbf{ranking quality}: AUROC for separating correct from incorrect outputs,
area under the risk-coverage curve, rank correlation against loss. Distribution-free risk control
\cite{angelopoulos2024crc} reports \textbf{certified acceptance}: the fraction of inputs a policy
may accept while a finite-sample bound holds on the error rate among accepted inputs.

These are not the same quantity. A score can order a population well on average and still fail to
isolate a clean prefix, and even a perfectly ordered prefix can be too small to certify. This paper
reports a case where a respectable AUROC coexists with zero certified acceptance, and separates the
reasons.

\paragraph{What we contribute.} We state up front what is \emph{not} new. That conformal risk
control frequently cannot certify LLM outputs, that base risk $\mu$ forces abstention of at least
$(\mu-\alpha)/(1-\alpha)$, and that this bites across multiple systems, are all established by
\citet{kotte2026crc}, which we read on 2026-07-18 and folded into this study's design three days
before its parameters were frozen. Roughly 80\% of what we measured is a replication of that work
in a new task. Three things are ours:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A protocol} (\S\ref{sec:protocol}): a frozen, sealed-split certification for
  event-argument extraction at the \textbf{source-document} unit, with mean bounded frame loss as
  the document loss. \citet{kotte2026crc} evaluates no event-argument extraction; we found no prior
  application of distribution-free risk control at this unit for this task, though we have not run
  a systematic survey.
\item \textbf{A three-way decomposition of the failure} (\S\ref{sec:three}), which is the paper's
  main finding. The third mechanism --- a well-ranked score defeated by the finite-sample penalty
  alone --- appears in only one of our three systems and is the one we had no vocabulary for.
\item \textbf{A dissociation between grounding and certifiability} (\S\ref{sec:grounding}),
  measured rather than asserted.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Task and unit.} RAMS \cite{ebner2020rams}, in-domain. A system may \textbf{abstain} on a
document rather than emit event frames. The independent unit is the \textbf{source document}, which
we call a \emph{group} because one source document carries one or more trigger instances: it is the
level at which the corpus has near-duplicates, so it is the level at which independence is
defensible. \textbf{Group loss} is the mean bounded frame loss over the document's triggers
($1 - $ span-exact micro-F1 per frame); a structurally invalid or ungrounded output scores 1.0.
\textbf{Group score} is the \textbf{minimum} confidence over the group's instances, and a group is
eligible only if \textbf{every} instance parsed and grounded. Accepting on the weakest member and
averaging the loss is what makes acceptance a statement about the whole document --- and
\S\ref{sec:three} shows it is also consequential.

\paragraph{Target, frozen before any sealed label was read.} Risk $\alpha = 0.20$, confidence
$\delta = 0.05$, acceptance floor 0.20. A commitment this study made, not a natural constant.
Nothing below relaxes it and no certificate is issued at any other operating point.

\paragraph{Systems.} Zero-shot (ZS), few-shot (FS), and a QLoRA-adapted (QA)
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, all served on one frozen 4-bit vLLM stack. Confidence is mean transition
log-probability over the parsed object; two alternatives were pre-registered and are reported.

\paragraph{Discipline.} The certification split was opened \textbf{exactly once} --- 3,152
documents forming 1,396 groups --- under a hash-chained access log, with zero reads of the held-out
\texttt{final} and \texttt{shift} splits, which remain sealed. Certification and all analysis ran
on CPU at no cost; the GPU programme totalled about \$18.7--18.9.

\section{The null, and three ways to reach it}
\label{sec:three}

\textbf{No system certifies.} All three are infeasible under the primary construction and under a
conservative audit. Because both baselines are infeasible, the two pre-registered comparisons
collapse to \textbf{one} finding, and no numerical difference is computed against an infeasible
baseline.

That much is a replication. What it does not say is \emph{why}, and answering that needs three
upper bounds on what any policy could have accepted, in increasing strictness
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). Confusing them is easy and consequential; an earlier draft of this paper
did exactly that, and \S\ref{sec:limitations} records it.

\paragraph{Mechanism 1: base risk.} For ZS and FS this alone settles it. Base risks of 0.992 and
0.958 permit at most 0.96\% and 5.25\% acceptance against a 20\% floor, so \emph{no score whatever}
could certify them, and their rows need no further explanation. It does \textbf{not} settle QA,
which is permitted 49.1\%. Attributing QA's null to $\mu$ is the error of treating a population
ceiling as a forecast --- the ceiling bounds what an oracle score could do, and nothing more.

\paragraph{Mechanism 2: ranking.} QA's score reaches 2.0\% acceptance where a perfect ranking of
the identical population reaches 23.6\%. Both figures are empirical and carry no finite-sample
penalty, so the comparison is like-for-like and the \textbf{21.6-point gap is attributable to the
ranking alone}. This is the score-discrimination result, and it is the only one of the three
mechanisms that is the score's fault.

What makes it worth reporting is that the score is not bad. Group AUROC for error detection is
\textbf{0.796}; $\rho = -0.403$ at $p \approx 10^{-55}$; normalized AURC 0.531 says it closes
roughly half the distance between a random ranking and a perfect one. On development the figures
are 0.834, $-0.313$ and 0.568. Table 5 of \citet{kotte2026crc} puts that in context: log-probability
scores there reach AUROC 0.485--0.604 across NER, QA and classification, but \textbf{0.793 on JSON
extraction}, the one structured-generation task in that paper. Our 0.796 sits almost exactly on it.
Two independent studies find this score family discriminating errors at close to 0.80 on structured
generation --- and the certification outcomes diverge completely, because JSON extraction has base
risk 0.033 and ours 0.6071. (Not like-for-like: theirs is instance-level, ours group-level.)

\paragraph{Mechanism 3: the finite-sample penalty.} Ranking well is not enough, and this is the
mechanism we had no vocabulary for. Take the perfect ranking of mechanism 2 --- 23.6\% acceptance,
empirical risk 0.174 at the floor --- and require it to \emph{certify} rather than merely to
average below $\alpha$. Its Hoeffding upper bound at the per-system $\delta/3$ allocation is
0.267 at the floor, and the largest acceptance it can certify is \textbf{11.4\%} (8.4\%
family-wide). \textbf{A perfect ranking of this population does not clear the floor either.} The
measured score certifies \textbf{0.0\%}.

QA is the only system in which this mechanism is visible, because ZS and FS are already dead at
mechanism 1. Figure~\ref{fig:rc} shows all three distances in one panel.

\paragraph{Taken together: overdetermined.} Each mechanism independently forbids the target. This
matters for what may be concluded. A reader shown only mechanism 2 would infer that a better score
would have certified this system; it would not. A reader shown only mechanism 1 would infer that
the model is simply too weak; that is false for QA. The honest statement is that
\textbf{nothing could have certified this system --- not a better score, not a perfect one} --- and
that the score is nonetheless responsible for a 21.6-point share of the shortfall.

\paragraph{The unit definition is also decisive}, and this is easy to miss. Stratifying QA's groups
by triggers per document, mean group loss is essentially flat (0.606, 0.608, 0.589, 0.634, 0.596
from 1 to 6+) --- but the share of groups within $\alpha$ collapses from \textbf{17.3\% to 0.7\% to
zero}, and 23 of the 28 oracle-accepted groups are single-trigger. Groups with three or more
triggers contribute \textbf{none}. Averaging frame loss across a document and scoring the group by
its weakest member makes acceptance vanish with document complexity even where mean loss does not
move. Cross-sentence distance behaves the same way: 25 of 28 acceptances have no cross-sentence
argument. A protocol reporting only aggregate loss sees neither effect.

\section{What the adapter changed, and what it did not}
\label{sec:grounding}

QLoRA adaptation produced a large, real improvement in \textbf{grounding fidelity}: 95.7\% of QA
outputs parse and ground to the source document against 39.4\% zero-shot and 68.1\% few-shot, with
ungrounded spans falling from 5,355 to 340. ``Valid'' here means parseable and grounded,
\textbf{not correct} --- a span can point at real document text and still name the wrong entity or
role --- and \textbf{this improvement did not translate into a certified selective guarantee}.
Neither half of that sentence may be reported without the other.

Decomposing loss into four channels says why. The ${\sim}16\times$ separation in ungrounded spans
lives in the channels forced to loss 1.0 or contentless. The certificate scores the fourth ---
well-formed, grounded, content-bearing output --- which holds \textbf{90.3\%} of QA's documents and
\textbf{87\%} of its loss, at a within-channel mean loss of 0.579. The adapter moved the channels
the certificate does not score and left the one it does.

\textbf{This is a dissociation, not an orthogonality result.} One intervention across three systems
cannot establish that two properties are independent, and we did not test independence. At group
level the two are in fact weakly associated ($\rho = -0.206$, $p \approx 8\times10^{-15}$) --- but
the 1,266 QA groups whose every member is grounded still average \textbf{0.589} loss, roughly three
times the target. Grounding was necessary and nowhere near sufficient.

The decomposition locates loss by channel; it does \textbf{not} identify the error category, and we
claim none. An earlier version attributed the residual to output ``that names the wrong entity'';
that rested on inspection of two development examples and was withdrawn on review. A measurement
points elsewhere: false positives exceed false negatives by 637 arguments, which is
over-generation.

\section{Relation to prior work}

\citet{kotte2026crc} establishes the impossibility bound, the base-risk feasibility test, and
infeasibility across multiple systems; we reproduce all three at a new task and unit, and add the
separation of mechanism 2 from mechanism 3. Our base risk (0.6071) is \textbf{not} a reproduction
of their extraction figure and does not contradict it: JSON-Extract sits at $\mu = 0.033$, a
different task under a different metric.

\textbf{This is not a task-wide impossibility.} On the datasets \citet{kotte2026crc} reports,
$(\mu-\alpha)/(1-\alpha)$ forbids nothing at $\mu = 0.033$ and permits up to 80.5\% acceptance at
$\mu = 0.356$, so a 20\% target is \emph{not excluded} on structured extraction at those base
risks. Not being forbidden is not achievability --- that paper finds certification practical only
at $\alpha = 0.30$--$0.40$ on its own tasks --- but it is enough to show the barrier here is
specific to this model, score and task.

\section{Conclusion}

A confidence score with AUROC 0.796 and $\rho = -0.403$ certified nothing on document-level
event-argument extraction, and neither would a perfect ranking of the same data. Infeasibility was
overdetermined by base risk, ranking and the finite-sample penalty together, and only the second is
the score's fault. Reporting ranking quality alone --- as selective-prediction work generally does
--- cannot distinguish these, and on this task it would have been misleading about all three.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{We made this paper's own error, twice.} A no-penalty empirical quantity was read as if it
were certifiable: caught for the $\alpha$-frontier point 0.406, missed for the oracle acceptance
0.2357, where a draft concluded that a perfect ranking ``would clear the floor''. It does not
(11.4\% certified against a 20\% floor); an independent review caught it. Both slips ran in the
direction that flattered the result. We report this because it is the paper's thesis happening to
its authors: the empirical figure looked like evidence of certifiability and was not.

\textbf{Not production-grade}, and no deployment claim is made. \textbf{The certificate bounds
average within-group loss, not the absence of error} in any document; certified $\neq$ safe.

\textbf{The system is not competitive on extraction quality.} Group loss 0.6071 corresponds to a
mean per-document span-exact F1 of about 0.393 --- a mean of per-document F1, \textbf{not}
comparable to corpus-level micro Arg-C figures such as the 54.4 of \citet{huang2026rose}, but the
gap is real and we claim no competitiveness.

\textbf{Single seed, no ablations.} One frozen 4-bit vLLM stack, 86.3\% parsed-output agreement
against the training stack, with the abstain/extract decision flipping between stacks at 1.3--1.8\%
of frames. Portability is not established.

\textbf{In-domain RAMS only}, span-exact matching; \texttt{final} and \texttt{shift} are descoped
and remain sealed, so no shift claim is made in either direction. \textbf{The mechanism-2 and
mechanism-3 quantities are post-hoc on an opened split}; they select no threshold and certify
nothing. \textbf{The infeasibility was knowable for \$0} before that split was opened, and neither
implementer nor reviewer ran the check. \textbf{Only one score family was tested}: agreement-based
scores reach AUROC 0.600--0.816 across the eight datasets of \citet{kotte2026crc} and might behave
differently, and mechanism 3 would still apply to them.

\section*{Reproducibility}

Protocol, certifier, diagnostics and claim ledger are code, and every figure resolves to a
committed artifact. Released with publication: source, the frozen group mapping, per-system
predictions with confidence candidates, and the certification and diagnostic artifacts. Raw corpus
text and sealed labels are not redistributable.
