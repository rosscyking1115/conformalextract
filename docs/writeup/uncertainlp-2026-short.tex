\title{A well-ranked score that certifies nothing:\\
selective risk control for document-level event-argument extraction}

\author{Anonymous submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Selective prediction is usually judged by how well a confidence score \emph{ranks} correct outputs
above incorrect ones --- AUROC, AURC, rank correlation. Distribution-free risk control asks a
different question: how much output can be accepted while provably bounding error on what is
accepted. We show these can diverge by an order of magnitude on the same system. We define a
frozen, sealed-split selective-certification protocol for document-level event-argument extraction
on RAMS, with the source document as the independent unit, and run it once on a QLoRA-adapted
Qwen3-4B and two prompted baselines. All three are infeasible at a 20\% risk target with 95\%
confidence and a 20\% acceptance floor, and the infeasibility is \textbf{overdetermined} by three
separate barriers --- base risk, ranking quality, the finite-sample penalty --- no one of which
could be removed to rescue it. They do not all bind everywhere: base risk settles the two baselines
and not the adapted model, which the other two settle between them. The
adapted system's confidence score is \emph{not} weak by selective-prediction standards --- group
AUROC 0.796, Spearman $\rho = -0.403$, close to the one log-probability figure reported elsewhere
for structured generation --- and it certifies \textbf{nothing at all}. Nor would a perfect ranking:
ranked by true loss, the same population certifies 11.4\%, short of the acceptance floor. Nor
would more data at the same operating point, and the reason is structural rather than a matter of
degree: acceptance is tested on a \emph{lower} confidence bound, so accepting exactly 20\% of a
corpus never clears a 20\% floor \textbf{at any corpus size}. A certificate requires coverage
strictly above the floor, and on this loss profile the smallest corpus admitting one is of order
\textbf{21$\times$} this split. The third mechanism is
observable in only one of our three systems, because the other two fail at base risk first. Separating the
three mechanisms is what makes the null diagnostic rather than merely negative, and only one of
them is the score's fault.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Two literatures measure confidence and rarely meet. Selective prediction and hallucination
detection report \textbf{ranking quality}: AUROC for separating correct from incorrect outputs,
area under the risk-coverage curve, rank correlation against loss. Distribution-free risk control
\cite{angelopoulos2024crc} reports \textbf{certified acceptance}: the fraction of inputs a policy
may accept while a finite-sample bound holds on the error rate among accepted inputs.

These are not the same quantity. A score can order a population well on average and still fail to
isolate a clean prefix, and even a perfectly ordered prefix can be too small to certify. This paper
reports a case where a respectable AUROC coexists with zero certified acceptance, and separates the
reasons.

\paragraph{What we contribute.}\label{sec:intro-contrib} We state up front what is \emph{not} new. That conformal risk
control frequently cannot certify LLM outputs, that base risk $\mu$ forces abstention of at least
$(\mu-\alpha)/(1-\alpha)$, and that this bites across multiple systems, are all established by
\citet{kotte2026crc}, which we read on 2026-07-18 and folded into this study's design three days
before its parameters were frozen. Most of what we measure replicates that work in a new task.
Three things are ours:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A protocol} (\S\ref{sec:protocol}): a frozen, sealed-split certification for
  event-argument extraction at the \textbf{source-document} unit. \citet{kotte2026crc} evaluates no
  event-argument extraction, and we found no prior application of distribution-free risk control at
  this unit for this task, though we ran no systematic survey.
\item \textbf{A three-way decomposition of the failure} (\S\ref{sec:three}), which is the paper's
  main finding. The third mechanism --- a well-ranked score defeated by the finite-sample penalty
  alone --- appears in only one of our three systems and is the one we had no vocabulary for.
\item \textbf{A dissociation between grounding and certifiability} (\S\ref{sec:grounding}),
  measured rather than asserted.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Task and unit.} RAMS \cite{ebner2020rams}, in-domain. A system may \textbf{abstain} on a
document rather than emit event frames. The independent unit is the \textbf{source document}, which
we call a \emph{group} because one source document carries one or more trigger instances: it is the
level at which the corpus has near-duplicates, so it is the level at which independence is
defensible. \textbf{Group loss} is the mean bounded frame loss over the document's triggers
($1 - $ span-exact micro-F1 per frame); a structurally invalid or ungrounded output scores 1.0.
\textbf{Group score} is the \textbf{minimum} confidence over the group's instances, and a group is
eligible only if \textbf{every} instance parsed and grounded. Accepting on the weakest member and
averaging the loss is what makes acceptance a statement about the whole document --- and
\S\ref{sec:three} shows it is also consequential.

\paragraph{Target, frozen before any sealed label was read.} Risk $\alpha = 0.20$, confidence
$\delta = 0.05$, acceptance floor 0.20. A commitment this study made, not a natural constant.
Nothing below relaxes it and no certificate is issued at any other operating point.

\paragraph{Systems.} Zero-shot (ZS), few-shot (FS), and a QLoRA-adapted (QA)
Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, all served on one frozen 4-bit vLLM stack. Confidence is mean transition
log-probability over the parsed object; two alternatives were pre-registered and are reported.

\paragraph{Discipline.} The certification split was opened \textbf{exactly once} --- 3,152
documents forming 1,396 groups --- under a hash-chained access log, with zero reads of the held-out
\texttt{final} and \texttt{shift} splits, which remain sealed. ``Zero reads'' means no gold from those splits
entered the access log or scoring; RAMS stores text and labels together, so the archive itself was
parsed whole. Certification and all analysis ran
on CPU at no cost; the GPU programme totalled \$18.7--18.9 --- a band rather than a figure because
there is no single spend ledger, and one job's cost is recovered by differencing running totals
that admit two readings.

\paragraph{Three population sizes, kept distinct.} 1{,}396 groups form the coverage denominator and
are what the certificate's sample precondition is checked against; \textbf{1{,}392} can be scored
and ranked, and that is the population every AUROC here uses; acceptance additionally requires
every member to parse and ground, leaving 1{,}266 for QA. \textbf{The four-group difference is not
incidental.} Each of the four contains a document that produced no generation at all, so it carries
no confidence value, and since a group scores at its weakest member the whole group is unrankable.
They are the \emph{same four in every system}, which makes them a property of the corpus rather
than of any model, and they remain rows --- so the precondition still sees 1{,}396 while no score
can order them.

\section{The null, and three ways to reach it}
\label{sec:three}

\textbf{No system certifies.} All three are infeasible under the primary construction and under a
conservative audit. Because both baselines are infeasible, the two pre-registered comparisons
collapse to \textbf{one} finding, and no numerical difference is computed against an infeasible
baseline.

That much is a replication. What it does not say is \emph{why}, and answering that needs three
upper bounds on what any policy could have accepted, in increasing strictness
(Table~\ref{tab:main}). Confusing them is easy and consequential; an earlier draft of this paper
did exactly that, and \S\ref{sec:limitations} records it.

\paragraph{Mechanism 1: base risk.} For ZS and FS this alone settles it. Base risks of 0.992 and
0.958 permit at most 0.96\% and 5.25\% acceptance against a 20\% floor, so \emph{no score whatever}
could certify them, and their rows need no further explanation. It does \textbf{not} settle QA,
which is permitted 49.1\%. Attributing QA's null to $\mu$ is the error of treating a population
ceiling as a forecast --- the ceiling bounds what an oracle score could do, and nothing more.

\paragraph{Mechanism 2: ranking.} QA's score reaches 2.0\% acceptance where a perfect ranking of
the identical population reaches 23.6\%. Both figures are empirical and carry no finite-sample
penalty, so the comparison is like-for-like and the \textbf{21.6-point gap is attributable to the
ranking alone}. This is the score-discrimination result, and it is the only one of the three
mechanisms that is the score's fault.

What makes it worth reporting is that the score is not bad. Group AUROC for error detection is
\textbf{0.796} (95\% bootstrap CI $[0.737, 0.850]$, 10{,}000 resamples; the interval is wide
because only 90 of 1{,}392 scored groups are error-free); $\rho = -0.403$ at $p \approx 10^{-55}$;
normalized AURC 0.531 says it closes roughly half the distance between a random ranking and a
perfect one. On development the figures are 0.834, $-0.313$ and 0.568. Table 5 of
\citet{kotte2026crc} puts that in the same broad range: log-probability scores there reach AUROC
0.485--0.604 across NER, QA and classification, and 0.793 on JSON extraction, the one
structured-generation task in that paper. We do not treat 0.796 against 0.793 as a matched
comparison --- theirs is instance-level and ours group-level, and our own interval spans
0.737--0.850. The claim we need is weaker and safe: this is an ordinary log-probability score,
performing about as its family does, not a straw man built to fail.

\paragraph{Mechanism 3: the finite-sample penalty.} Ranking well is not enough, and this is the
mechanism we had no vocabulary for. \textbf{Two thresholds must be kept apart, and we name them
separately because conflating them cost us three published figures.} The
\emph{20\%-coverage prefix} is the smallest accepted set that is 20\% of the corpus:
$0.20 \times 1{,}396 = 279.2$, so it is $k = \mathbf{280}$, and every figure in this paragraph uses
it. The \emph{acceptance floor} is the protocol's own test, applied to a lower confidence bound
rather than to raw coverage, and it is not met until $k = 332$ (\S\ref{sec:frozen-config}). Take
the perfect ranking of mechanism 2 --- 23.6\% acceptance, empirical risk 0.175 at the
20\%-coverage prefix --- and require it to \emph{certify} rather than merely to average below
$\alpha$. Its Hoeffding upper bound at the per-system $\delta/3$ allocation is 0.267 there, and the
largest acceptance it can certify is \textbf{11.4\%} (8.4\% family-wide). \textbf{A perfect ranking
of this population does not reach the acceptance floor either.} The measured score certifies
\textbf{0.0\%}.

\paragraph{Which bound, and why it matters.} The 11.4\% is the \emph{conservative audit's}
Hoeffding bound, not the primary certificate, whose risk test is an \textbf{empirical Bernstein}
bound --- the natural objection here, since a loss-optimal prefix is almost all zero-loss and
Bernstein is tighter on low variance. It does not rescue the result: run through the certifier
itself with one pre-chosen oracle candidate and no multiplicity penalty, that ranking certifies
\textbf{0.0000} at every coverage tested. So 11.4\% is the \emph{most generous} certified reading
available.

\paragraph{At the frozen configuration, both constraints fail.}\label{sec:frozen-config} A
certificate must clear the acceptance floor \emph{and} the risk target, and at this corpus size the
perfect ranking clears neither. The frozen allocation is three candidates per system at $\delta/3$ --- both divisions
apply --- and there the oracle prefix's acceptance lower bound is \textbf{0.1984}, \emph{below} the
0.20 floor; the smallest prefix that would clear it is 332 against the oracle's 329. The risk test
fails independently. We state this because an earlier draft of this paragraph quoted 0.2017, which
is the same bound computed without the per-system $\delta/3$ division, and concluded that
acceptance cleared and only risk bound --- a stronger-sounding claim that is false at the
configuration it named. Every figure in this paragraph is emitted with its allocation in the
released sensitivity artifact.

QA is the only system in which this mechanism is visible, because ZS and FS are already dead at
mechanism 1. Figure~\ref{fig:rc} shows all three distances in one panel.

\paragraph{The corpus is too small to certify this target, and that is a fact about the
benchmark.} Mechanism 3 is not a property of this run. Two independent calculations say so, and
they are separate arguments rather than one restated.

\emph{From the bound.} At $n = 1{,}396$ and this study's $\delta/3$ allocation, the Hoeffding
radius at the floor is \textbf{0.0925}, spent before any data is seen --- so passing the
\emph{risk test} at the floor requires an accepted set carrying mean loss $\leq \mathbf{0.1075}$.
\textbf{At this allocation the benchmark halves the effective risk target}, for any system. It does
not follow that such a system certifies: a certificate must also clear the acceptance floor on its
\emph{lower} bound, which at the floor-meeting prefix it does not, and 332 accepted groups are
needed for that. Clearing the risk test with \emph{this} loss distribution would take
\textbf{18{,}740 groups, 13.4$\times$ this split}. Both figures are properties of $n$ and of the
$\delta/3$ split, not of the corpus alone: a single-system entrant at the full $\delta$ inherits
neither.

\emph{From the certifier's own precondition, which binds far sooner.} At the frozen $\delta/3$
allocation the theorem admits a grid of at most \textbf{three} candidates on this corpus: three
require 1{,}386 groups and the split holds 1{,}396, but a fourth would require 1{,}432. \textbf{The
frozen grid is the largest this allocation can support}, and it is frozen at exactly that size. We
state capacity rather than a margin in groups because the row count and the rankable count differ
--- four groups contain a document that produced no generation at all, and since a group scores at
its weakest member all four are unscorable, the same four for every system, so a property of the
corpus rather than any model. They remain rows, so the precondition still sees 1{,}396; they cannot
be ranked, so 1{,}392 is what any score orders. \textbf{At a three-system $\delta/3$ split this
corpus sits at the edge of what the certificate can be run on at all} --- a constraint on the
benchmark under this design, applying to work that has not run yet. A study certifying one system
at the full $\delta$ would face a laxer capacity, and we do not claim otherwise.

\paragraph{Taken together: overdetermined.} Every system is blocked by at least one mechanism and
QA by two, so no single repair rescues any of them. The three are \emph{not} all active on every
system, and saying they \emph{each} forbid the target would be false: base risk permits QA 49.1\%
and settles only the two baselines.

That distinction decides what a reader may conclude, and either half alone misleads. Shown only
mechanism 2, a reader infers that a better score would have certified this system --- it would not;
the perfect ranking of the same population certifies 11.4\%. Shown only mechanism 1, a reader
infers that the model is simply too weak --- false for QA, whose base risk permits nearly half the
corpus. The honest statement needs both: \textbf{nothing could have certified this system --- not a
better score, not a perfect one} --- \emph{and} the score is still responsible for a 21.6-point
share of the shortfall. A null that names only one mechanism is not a weaker version of this
finding; it is a different and wrong one.

\paragraph{The unit definition is also decisive}, and it is not separable from mechanism 3: the
unit fixes the group count, which is exactly what the capacity argument above turns on. Stratifying
QA's groups by triggers per document, mean group loss is essentially flat --- 0.606, 0.608, 0.589,
0.634, 0.596 from one trigger to six or more --- but the share of groups within $\alpha$ collapses
from \textbf{17.3\% to 0.7\% to zero}, and 23 of the 28 oracle-accepted groups are single-trigger.
Groups with three or more triggers contribute \textbf{none}. Averaging frame loss across a document
and scoring the group by its weakest member makes acceptance vanish with document complexity
\emph{even where mean loss does not move}. Cross-sentence distance behaves the same way: 25 of 28
acceptances have no cross-sentence argument. \textbf{A protocol reporting only aggregate loss sees
neither effect}, and would read this corpus as uniformly hard rather than as hard in a way the
aggregation choice creates.

\section{What the adapter changed, and what it did not}
\label{sec:grounding}

QLoRA produced a large, real improvement in \textbf{grounding fidelity}: 95.7\% of QA outputs
parse and ground to the source document against 39.4\% zero-shot and 68.1\% few-shot, ungrounded
spans falling from 5,355 to 340. ``Valid'' means parseable and grounded, \textbf{not correct}, and
\textbf{this did not translate into a certified selective guarantee}; neither half of that sentence
may be reported without the other. Decomposing loss into four channels says why: the
${\sim}16\times$ separation lives in the channels forced to loss 1.0 or contentless, while the
certificate scores the fourth --- well-formed, grounded, content-bearing output, holding
\textbf{90.3\%} of QA's documents and \textbf{87\%} of its loss at a within-channel mean of 0.579.
The adapter moved the channels the certificate does not score.

\textbf{This is a dissociation, not an orthogonality result.} One intervention across three systems
cannot establish independence and we did not test it; at group level the two are weakly associated
($\rho = -0.206$, $p \approx 8\times10^{-15}$), yet the 1{,}266 fully-grounded QA groups still
average \textbf{0.589} loss, three times the target. Grounding was necessary and nowhere near
sufficient. The decomposition locates loss by channel and identifies no error category: an earlier
version blamed output ``that names the wrong entity'' on two development examples and was withdrawn
on review, and a measurement points elsewhere --- false positives exceed false negatives by 637
arguments, which is over-generation.

\section{Relation to prior work}

What we add to \citet{kotte2026crc} (\S\ref{sec:intro-contrib}) is the separation of mechanism 2
from mechanism 3. One boundary: our base risk (0.6071) is \textbf{not} a reproduction of their
extraction figure and does not contradict it --- JSON-Extract sits at $\mu = 0.033$, a different
task under a different metric.

\textbf{This is not a task-wide impossibility.} On their datasets $(\mu-\alpha)/(1-\alpha)$ forbids
nothing at $\mu = 0.033$ and permits up to 80.5\% acceptance at $\mu = 0.356$, so a 20\% target is
\emph{not excluded} on structured extraction. Not being forbidden is not achievability --- they
find certification practical only at $\alpha = 0.30$--$0.40$ --- but it shows the barrier here is
specific to this model, score and task.

\section{Conclusion}

A score with AUROC 0.796 certified nothing here, and neither would a perfect ranking of the same
data. Infeasibility was overdetermined --- base risk settles the two baselines, ranking and the
finite-sample penalty settle the adapted model, and no single repair rescues any of the three ---
and only ranking is the score's fault. Ranking quality alone distinguishes
none of them.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Our publication gate cannot see the error this paper is about, and we made that error five
times in the claims reported here.} That scope is stated rather than implied: five is the count
within the reported claims, not the count in the project, and an unscoped five would be doing the
thing this section is about. Start with the gate's design, because the record below follows from
it. The gate checks
that a claim's cited evidence exists and is version-controlled. It never opens the file, never
compares a number against it, and never sees the configuration --- the confidence allocation, the
candidate count, the sample size --- that produced either. Three things follow immediately. The
defect it is blind to is \emph{quoting a quantity that resembles a certified one and is not}: the
citation resolves, so the claim passes, and a figure computed at one configuration and written into
prose naming another is indistinguishable to it from a correct one. Nothing bounds how often that
happens, because nothing is looking. And the only filter left is whether a number looks plausible
to its author --- which is not a neutral filter, because a number that flatters the result looks
plausible to the author who wants it.

\textbf{The five instances are what that design predicts.} (i) The $\alpha$-frontier point 0.406
was nearly reported as a threshold; caught by a claim-wording audit. (ii) The empirical oracle
0.2357 was reported as clearing the floor; caught by independent review, and false --- certified,
it is 11.4\%. (iii) That 11.4\% was labelled ``certified'' without saying it is the conservative
audit's bound rather than the primary construction; caught by a pre-submission audit. (iv) A
claim-narrowing correction was applied to a derived document and not to the claim ledger it derives
from, so a byte-identity guard propagated the uncorrected text into the generated appendix ---
caught by review. (v) An acceptance lower bound was quoted at an allocation the paper did not use,
supporting a sentence that is false at the allocation it named --- caught by review. Every one of
the five cites evidence that exists and is tracked. The gate returned \emph{ok} for all of them.

\textbf{All five erred in the direction that flattered the result, and the last two were caught by
reviewers rather than by our own checking.} That is the predicted bias, observed. Instances (iv)
and (v) occurred inside passages written specifically to state a margin honestly: the intention to
be careful did not prevent them, because intention is not the variable --- what varies is whether
anything reads the evidence. We report this as a measurement rather than a confession. Five
instances of one defect class in the reported claims, all in one direction, under a gate that by
construction cannot see any of them, is a property of the gate. \textbf{Our gate certifies agreement, not correctness},
which is the same distinction, one level up, that this paper draws between ranking a population
well and certifying anything about it.

\textbf{Not production-grade}, and no deployment claim is made. \textbf{The certificate bounds
average within-group loss, not the absence of error} in any document; certified $\neq$ safe.

\textbf{The system is not competitive on extraction quality.} Group loss 0.6071 corresponds to a
mean per-document span-exact F1 of about 0.393 --- a mean of per-document F1, \textbf{not}
comparable to corpus-level micro Arg-C figures such as the 54.4 of \citet{huang2026rose}, but the
gap is real and we claim no competitiveness.

\textbf{Single seed, no ablations.} One frozen 4-bit vLLM stack, 86.3\% parsed-output agreement
against the training stack. The abstain/extract decision flips between stacks on \textbf{1.3\%} of
frames, and those flips \emph{concentrate} rather than thin out under tightening --- \textbf{1.8\%}
among the most-confident three quarters. These are two measurements, not an interval: the same
flips over a smaller denominator. Portability is not established.

\textbf{In-domain RAMS only}, span-exact matching; \texttt{final} and \texttt{shift} are descoped
and remain sealed, so no shift claim is made in either direction. \textbf{The mechanism-2 and
mechanism-3 quantities are post-hoc on an opened split}; they select no threshold and certify
nothing. \textbf{The infeasibility was knowable for \$0} before that split was opened, and neither
implementer nor reviewer ran the check. \textbf{Only one score family was tested}: agreement-based
scores reach AUROC 0.600--0.816 across the eight datasets of \citet{kotte2026crc} and might behave
differently, and mechanism 3 would still apply to them.

\section*{Reproducibility}

Protocol, certifier, diagnostics and claim ledger are code. \textbf{Every figure in this paper is
checked mechanically against a committed artifact carrying both its value and the configuration
that produced it} --- the $\delta$, its allocation, the candidate count, the $n$ --- with the
exceptions named in the released manifest. A figure carrying no configuration is how three of the
errors in Limitations happened. Artifact hashes are pinned over
git-canonical (LF) bytes and say so: \textbf{a hash without its byte convention is the same defect
as a bound without its $\delta$ allocation}, and we shipped one --- a CRLF working-copy digest,
which no reader on a Linux checkout could reproduce, in a line whose only purpose was to let them
try. Released with publication: source, the frozen group mapping, per-system
predictions with confidence candidates, and the certification and diagnostic artifacts. Raw corpus
text and sealed labels are not redistributable.
